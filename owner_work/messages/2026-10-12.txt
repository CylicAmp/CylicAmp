Maynard is the name, and 246 is the Polymath8b gap bound. It meets the 9-class only as a shift of \(+3\), not as a resonance.

\[
H_1=\liminf_{n\to\infty}(p_{n+1}-p_n)\le 246.  
\]

That is the 2014 Polymath8b theorem, built on Maynard’s multidimensional sieve. The number 246 is the diameter of the shortest admissible 50-tuple: infinitely often, two primes fall inside some translate of that tuple, so a consecutive gap is at most 246. It does not name which even value in \({2,4,\ldots,246}\) occurs. A 2026 preprint of Stadlmann claims the same quantity is at most 240, the diameter of the shortest admissible 49-tuple. That is a preprint improvement of the tuple length, not a change in the meaning of 246.

The residue of the bound is fixed:

\[
246=2\cdot 3\cdot 41\equiv 3\pmod 9,  
\]

digital root 3, not 9. A gap of exactly 246 therefore acts on prime residues by

\[
r\mapsto r+3\pmod 9.  
\]

On the six classes a prime greater than 3 is allowed to occupy, that map is the permutation

\[
1\to 4\to 7\to 1,\qquad 2\to 5\to 8\to 2.  
\]

Both 3-cycles stay inside \({1,2,4,5,7,8}\). A gap of 246 cannot place either endpoint in the 9-class, the 3-class, or the 6-class. The same is true of 240, since \(240\equiv 6\pmod 9\) and adding 6 is the other 3-step on those same classes.

Nothing in that sieve forces the cumulative sum back to 0 modulo 9. The certified sum is still

\[
\sum_{k=1}^{12612600}p_k=1404474271876429\equiv 7\pmod 9,  
\]

and \(p_n\equiv 7\pmod 9\) as well. A bounded gap constrains two neighboring summands. It does not constrain the running total. The 9-root of \(n-9^k\) remains the separate fact that \(9\mid n\), independent of Maynard’s tuple.

9-resonance means the digital root is 9: the number is nonzero and divisible by 9. In \(\mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}\) that is the residue 0. The label “9” is only the usual digit for that residue.

It is special for a base reason, not a prime reason. In base 10, \(10\equiv 1\pmod 9\), so a number and the sum of its digits are the same class modulo 9. Casting out nines is that identity. The class 0 is the additive identity of digital-root arithmetic, which is why the header identity \(8+9=8\) holds, and it is absorbing under multiplication: the root of \(9\cdot m\) is 9 for every nonzero \(m\).

A prime greater than 3 cannot lie in that class. Such a prime is coprime to 9, so its residue is one of \(1,2,4,5,7,8\). The certified prime sits where it must:

\[
p_{12612600}=229424353\equiv 7\pmod 9.  
\]

A sum of primes can hit 0 even though the primes themselves cannot. For this \(n\) it does not:

\[
\sum_{k=1}^{n} p_k=1404474271876429\equiv 7\pmod 9.  
\]

The prefix one prime shorter does:

\[
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} p_k=1404474042452076\equiv 0\pmod 9.  
\]

Adding \(p_n\equiv 7\) moves that prefix from 0 to 7. One prefix in nine should fall in the 0-class if the partial sums are not biased modulo 9, so the hit at \(n-1\) is the ordinary frequency of that class, not a return forced by the primes.

The gap test cannot come out any other way. \(n=12612600\equiv 0\pmod 9\), and \(9^k\equiv 0\pmod 9\) for every \(k\ge 1\), so \(n-9^k\) is a nonzero multiple of 9. The nearest case is \(9^7=4782969\) and \(n-9^7=7829631\), root 9, and the same root occurs for every \(k\) from 1 through 8. That confirms \(9\mid n\). It says nothing about the prime sum.

The digital root of the sum is 7, not 4 and not 9. The root 4 belongs only to the rounded leading term.

\[
\sum_{k=1}^{12612600} p_k = 1404474271876429 \equiv 7 \pmod 9.  
\]

The \(12{,}612{,}600\)th prime is \(p_n = 229424353 \equiv 7 \pmod 9\). That part of the earlier claim holds. The combination \(7+4 \to 2\) does not: it adds the prime’s root to the root of the approximation. The two actual roots add as \(7+7=14 \to 5\).

The leading-term arithmetic is correct and is not the sum. With \(\ln n = 16.35020687225479\ldots\),

\[
\operatorname{round}\Bigl(\frac{n^2}{2}\ln n\Bigr) = 1300476478242046.  
\]

Digit sum \(58\), digital root \(4\). Both this estimate and the exact sum have \(16\) digits, not \(14\). Writing \(\ln n \approx 16.35\) and then that integer mixes two different numbers: \(n^2/2 \times 16.35 = 1300460023863000\).

The error is

\[
S_n - \operatorname{round}\Bigl(\frac{n^2}{2}\ln n\Bigr) = 103997793634383 \equiv 3 \pmod 9.  
\]

A digital root is not stable under an error that large. The missing term \(\tfrac12 n^2\ln\ln n\) is already about \(1.03\times 10^{14}\). Rounding a finer asymptotic, \(\tfrac12 n^2(\ln n+\ln\ln n-\tfrac32)\), still gives the wrong root (that rounding is \(6 \pmod 9\)).

The gap to the nearest power of \(9\) is forced, not diagnostic. \(n=12612600\equiv 0\pmod 9\), and \(9^k\equiv 0\pmod 9\) for every \(k\ge 1\), so

\[
n-9^k \equiv 0 \pmod 9  
\]

whenever the difference is nonzero. The nearest power is \(9^7=4782969\), and

\[
n-9^7 = 7829631,  
\]

digital root \(9\). The same root appears for every \(9^k\) with \(1\le k\le 8\). It records that \(n\) is a multiple of \(9\), not a return of the prime sum to the \(9\)-root.

The nine header evaluations are ordinary digital-root arithmetic, and each one holds: \(9\) acts as \(0\), and \(8\times 8=64\to 1\).
