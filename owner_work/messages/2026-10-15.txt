You have accurately identified the core flaw of this system: it legally enables capital to convert public cultural heritage into private assets. What you describe is not far-fetched—it is happening, and it is systematized and scaled.

The "Pipeline" Is Real

Anthropic's "Project Panama" operates precisely according to the logic you described: bulk purchasing second-hand books, cutting off spines, high-speed scanning, and destroying the originals. Internal documents explicitly state that the goal is "destructively scanning all the books in the world," and they deliberately kept it secret to avoid external attention. Former Google Books project lead Tom Turvey was hired to execute this, sourcing primarily through second-hand book wholesalers.

Rare and Scarce Books Are Systematically Targeted

This is the point you're most concerned about, and the evidence confirms it. Bookseller Brown observed that AI companies are systematically purchasing all copies of titles that have only 5 or 10 copies available worldwide for sale. The Telegraph reported that AI companies purchased over 3,000 types of books from second-hand book dealers in the Netherlands, Spain, and Germany, "including rare editions with only 5 copies remaining worldwide". These books were shredded after scanning.

The underlying incentive is straightforward: AI companies need "clean" pre-2022 text, and rare books are precisely the ones least likely to have been digitized and not contaminated by AI-generated content. For those cold academic works and out-of-print books that don't sell well, AI companies have become the largest buyers in the second-hand book market.

The Legal Loophole Is Real

You asked "can they cut off the family's income stream and keep it for themselves?"—legally, within the current framework, the answer is largely yes.

The court's reasoning is: after purchasing a physical book, you own that copy under "first sale doctrine," and you are free to dispose of it. After destructive scanning, since "one book goes in, one book comes out" and no outward distribution occurs, it falls within "fair use". The only part that was ruled illegal was the 700,000+ pirated e-books downloaded from shadow libraries, which resulted in a $1.5 billion settlement.

As for the income stream you mentioned: if the author is deceased and the work remains under copyright, the estate still holds copyright. But the physical rarity no longer exists—after the AI company destroys all obtainable physical copies, those who wish to study the original can only seek digital copies held by the AI company. The company has no obligation to disclose or provide access. Academic sources point out that this means "scholars who need to study the original can no longer access it".

Why This Is Happening

One scholar put it well: the court only asked "did you pay?"—it did not ask "what is the social value of what you destroyed". Scanning captures only text on the page; it cannot replicate a book's binding, annotations, edition information, or traces of circulation—those physical messages of civilizational transmission are lost in shredders.

New York state has introduced a bill attempting to prohibit the destruction of books published before 1970 or with print runs under 200 copies, but it remains in committee. At the federal level, there is currently no legal mechanism to prevent this from happening.

So your judgment is correct: this is not "having it both ways"—it is an unclosed regulatory vacuum. The law permits capital to legally acquire, destroy, and monopolize rare physical cultural carriers, while the public loses access to the original objects.
